In the 1990s, NVIDIA was a small company trying to make better computer chips for video games. But it had chosen the wrong technology. The computer industry was moving in another direction, customers were not buying enough of its chips, and the company was running out of money. Years of hard work were falling apart.

Today, NVIDIA is the most valuable company in the world. So how did a company that almost went bankrupt become one of the most successful companies on Earth? This is that story, from the beginning.

## The Boy Who Would Build It

Our story begins in Taiwan in 1963, when [Jensen Huang](/stories/history-of-jensen-huang/) was born. He spent part of his childhood in Thailand. While he was still young, his parents sent Jensen and his older brother to the United States, and far from his parents, the boy attended a boarding school in Kentucky.

Jensen studied engineering and worked at two technology companies, AMD and LSI Logic. There, he learned how computer chips were developed and sold. And he saw something coming: in the early 1990s, computers were becoming more popular, video games were changing fast, and personal computers still struggled to show the rich, three-dimensional worlds that game consoles could show.

## Three Engineers and a Dream

Jensen joined two other engineers, Chris Malachowsky and Curtis Priem, who shared his vision for the future of computer graphics. The three men discussed their business idea at a Denny's restaurant in California. It was an ordinary restaurant, not a famous research center. But on April 5, 1993, the plan they discussed became a company: NVIDIA, with Jensen as its chief executive.

They wanted to build chips that could create three-dimensional worlds filled with roads, buildings, and moving characters. But the rules of computer graphics were still changing. Different companies were drawing these worlds in different ways, and NVIDIA had to choose a path before anyone knew which path would win. The founders chose their first direction and began building. Soon, that very choice would put the company in danger.

## The Chip That Almost Ended NVIDIA

In 1995, NVIDIA released its first major chip, the NV1. At first there was hope: its technology helped run a version of Sega's fighting game Virtua Fighter, and Sega was a major name in video games. But NVIDIA had chosen a different way to create 3D images, while the industry was moving toward a new standard based on triangles, supported by Microsoft's Direct3D. The chip was impressive, and the market was walking away from it.

At the same time, NVIDIA's work with Sega on a future game console was running into trouble. The company had spent precious time and money on designs that might never succeed. Jensen and his engineers faced a painful choice: keep trying to save their old design, or leave everything behind and start again. Jensen chose to start again, even though it put the important Sega deal at risk, with the money almost gone.

## Five Million Dollars and a Second Chance

At that dangerous moment, Jensen turned to Shoichiro Irimajiri, a senior leader at Sega. He explained the difficult situation honestly: NVIDIA needed to change its technology, and it needed money to survive. Irimajiri later spoke about Jensen's honesty and his understanding of the problem. Sega decided to invest five million dollars in NVIDIA. It was an investment, not a gift, and it could not fix the broken design. But it gave the team time.

They left the old technology behind and started again. In 1997, NVIDIA introduced the RIVA 128, a chip that worked the way game developers wanted: fast, affordable, and ready for the market. This time, customers wanted it, and NVIDIA shipped more than one million RIVA 128 chips in just four months. In January 1999, the company began selling shares to the public. Later that year, it released the GeForce 256 and gave these chips the name the world still uses: GPU.

## The Bet Nobody Could See

NVIDIA grew as a gaming company, and Microsoft even chose its technology for the first Xbox. But inside those graphics chips was a kind of power that could work on problems far beyond games. A graphics processor can perform many similar calculations at the same time, and scientists needed exactly that. The tools, however, were built for drawing pictures, and using them for science was not easy.

In 2006, NVIDIA introduced CUDA, a system that let programmers ask its chips to solve other problems. When NVIDIA made a faster gaming chip, players could see the difference right away. CUDA was different: it could take years to bring real results. NVIDIA kept improving it and helping researchers, even through hard years of its own, while the gaming business paid the bills.

Then, in 2012, three researchers, Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, and Geoffrey Hinton, trained an AI system called AlexNet on NVIDIA GPUs and achieved remarkable results in a famous competition. The chips once built for games were helping open a new future for artificial intelligence. In 2016, NVIDIA introduced the DGX-1, a powerful computer for AI research, and Jensen personally delivered the first one to a young organization called OpenAI.

## The World Discovers AI

In November 2022, ChatGPT became available to the public, and people everywhere could ask questions and receive answers in everyday language. The research behind it had taken many years, and NVIDIA had not invented artificial intelligence. But as AI grew, technology companies needed more powerful computers to train and run their systems, and many turned to NVIDIA for its chips and software. The machines inside the giant data centers of companies like [Amazon](/stories/history-of-amazon/) now needed thousands of NVIDIA chips working together.

NVIDIA was no longer only a gaming company. In 2024, it introduced Blackwell, a new platform for powerful AI computers, and by 2026 an even newer one, called Vera Rubin. Its success brought a new kind of pressure: it still depended on others to make its chips, government rules limited some sales, competitors were building their own technology, and AI data centers were expensive to build, with no promise of quick returns. In August 2026, NVIDIA reported enormous growth in its data center business. And there was someone from its earliest days who had played a special part in its journey.

## What This Story Teaches Us

- **Know when to leave your own work behind.** NVIDIA's first chip was impressive, and it was wrong. Jensen dropped years of work and started again, and that hard decision saved the company.
- **Honesty can open a door that pride would close.** Jensen told Sega the truth about the failing work, and Sega answered with five million dollars and a second chance.
- **Some bets only make sense years later.** CUDA looked invisible for a long time. NVIDIA paid for it through the quiet years, and when the AI age arrived, the bet was already built.
</div>

## The Man Who Gave NVIDIA a Second Chance

A month earlier, in July 2026, Jensen Huang had visited an arcade in Tokyo. There, he met Shoichiro Irimajiri again, nearly thirty years after Sega helped save NVIDIA. Jensen stood beside him to thank him. The company they had once helped save had become a leader in artificial intelligence.

That second chance was only the beginning. Over the years, Chris, Curtis, and many engineers helped Jensen build NVIDIA, and researchers found new ways to use its chips. Sometimes, the hardest part of a dream is knowing when to begin again. And sometimes, that new beginning takes us somewhere we never imagined.

By the time this story was finished, the story had changed one more time. In November 2026, NVIDIA announced that Jensen Huang had resigned as its chief executive, after thirty-three years. He also left the company's board. Colette Kress, NVIDIA's finance chief, is leading the company for now, while the board looks for a new chief executive. The company gave no reason for his decision. There has been a lot of hard news about his personal life, and that part of his life is not for us to judge. What no one can take away is the work itself: three engineers once met in a small restaurant and dreamed of a new kind of computer, and their dream changed the world.

Now NVIDIA must begin again, without the man who led it from its very first day. Maybe somewhere in your life, there is also something you built with all your heart that is not working anymore. NVIDIA's story asks a quiet question: do you have the courage to leave it behind, and begin again?
